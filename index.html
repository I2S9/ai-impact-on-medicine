1. The project

AI is often said to be about to “revolutionize medicine”. To see where it is really used, we follow the medical devices that regulators have actually authorized.

Our main source is the FDA, the U.S. agency that must authorize a medical device before it can be sold in the United States. It publishes the only public list of AI-enabled medical devices.

  • 1,614 AI-enabled devices on the FDA list (June 2026)
  • ~76% of them are in radiology
  • 0 devices based on generative AI or LLMs (Sept 2026)
  • 2.4% of supporting clinical studies are randomized

Figures from Complizen (2026), The Medical Futurist (2026) and JAMA Network Open (2025). We will recompute them ourselves from the FDA data.

2. Research questions

Main question: Despite the narrative that AI “will revolutionize medicine”, in which medical fields is AI actually authorized and used?

  1. How has the number of authorized AI medical devices evolved?
  2. Why does radiology dominate?
  3. How large is the gap between published research, clinical trials and authorized products?
  4. Which incidents or failures have been recorded?

3. Intended audience

  • Health students and professionals: which AI tools are really authorized in their field?
  • General public interested in health: what does “AI in medicine” actually mean today?

For both audiences: one message per chart, plain-language annotations, a glossary for regulatory terms, and every number linked to its source.

4. Data at hand

  • FDA — AI-Enabled Medical Devices list: about 1,600 devices with decision date, specialty, company and clearance pathway (Q1, Q2).
  • openFDA — 510(k) API: applicant country and city, joined by submission number (Q1, Q2).
  • ClinicalTrials.gov — API v2: AI and machine-learning trials by year, phase, condition and country (Q3).
  • PubMed — E-utilities: number of “AI + specialty” publications per year (Q3).
  • openFDA — device recalls and MAUDE: recalls and adverse-event reports for AI devices (Q4).
  • AI Incident Database: documented AI failures, filterable for healthcare (Q4).

Known limits

  • The FDA list covers the United States only and is updated retroactively, so yearly counts differ between sources.
  • Europe has no complete public equivalent yet (EUDAMED is still incomplete).
  • An authorization does not tell us whether a device is actually used in hospitals.
  • MAUDE under-reports AI-specific failures; we cross-check with recalls and incident reports.

5. Visualizations

Planned pages, one per question:

  • Stacked area chart — authorizations by specialty over time (Q1)
  • Treemap — radiology's share of all AI devices (Q2)

  • World map — where devices are made and where AI trials take place (Q1, Q3)
  • Funnel — publications → clinical trials → authorized devices (Q3)
