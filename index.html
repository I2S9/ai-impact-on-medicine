AI at the Doctor's

Where is AI actually entering medicine?

Inspiration

Our course asks us to map the impact of AI through data. Most public conversations about that impact are made of forecasts, promises and fears, which are hard to check. We wanted a field where the impact of AI leaves a measurable and public trace, and medicine offers exactly that: before an AI tool can be used on patients in the United States, the FDA has to authorize it, and every authorization is recorded with a date, a medical specialty and a regulatory pathway.

Health is also where the impact of AI matters most directly. When a recommendation algorithm fails, a user sees the wrong video; when a diagnostic tool fails, a patient can receive the wrong diagnosis. Headlines regularly announce that AI will “revolutionize medicine”, yet few people know which tools are actually approved, in which specialties, and on what evidence. Showing the gap between this narrative and the authorized reality seemed both useful and timely.

Finally, the topic suits the tools of data visualization. The FDA list is open, regularly updated and rich enough to be compared across years, specialties and regulatory pathways, and it can be linked to clinical trials, scientific publications and safety reports. It turns a vague question, “is AI changing medicine?”, into precise questions that charts can answer.

Behind the hype: what is actually authorized?

AI is often said to be about to “revolutionize medicine”. To see where it is really used, we follow the medical devices that regulators have actually authorized.

  • 1,524 AI-enabled devices on the FDA list (snapshot of June 2026)
  • 76% of them are in radiology
  • 0 devices based on generative AI or LLMs (Sept 2026)
  • 2.4% of supporting clinical studies are randomized

FDA (U.S. Food and Drug Administration): the agency that must authorize a medical device before it can be sold in the United States. It publishes the only public list of AI-enabled medical devices, the backbone of our project.

Sources: FDA AI-Enabled Medical Devices list (snapshot of 29 June 2026), The Medical Futurist (2026) and JAMA Network Open (2025).

Known limits

US-only list

Updated retroactively, so yearly counts differ between sources.

No EU equivalent

EUDAMED is still incomplete: Europe is shown as a documented gap.

Authorized ≠ used

No data on real hospital use: we say “authorized”, not “adopted”.

Under-reported failures

MAUDE misses AI-specific errors: we cross-check recalls and incidents.
